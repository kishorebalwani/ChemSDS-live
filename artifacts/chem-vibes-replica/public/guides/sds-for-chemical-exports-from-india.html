For chemical manufacturers, formulators, and merchant exporters operating out of India, export safety documentation carries operational, logistical, and commercial importance. While non-hazardous substances and manufactured articles frequently move under Non-Dangerous Goods (NDG) declarations, products classified as hazardous substances or mixtures require an SDS aligned with destination port and customs rules.

The 16-section SDS structure is widely used internationally within GHS-based regulatory systems, while specific legal requirements are established by national and regional regulations.

The GHS Building Block Approach: Jurisdictional Divergence

Because countries adopt different revisions and "building blocks" of the UN GHS framework, an SDS prepared for domestic use cannot simply be reused without destination-specific adaptation:

  • United States: Governed by OSHA under the Hazard Communication Standard (29 CFR 1910.1200). Where applicable, OSHA Permissible Exposure Limits (PELs) and other relevant occupational exposure limits should be identified in Section 8, along with US-specific emergency contact and regulatory information.
  • European Union: Governed by Regulation (EU) 2020/878 (amending Annex II of REACH) and the CLP Regulation ((EC) No 1272/2008). For hazardous mixtures requiring notification to Poison Centres, a UFI may be required in the SDS and on the product label.
  • Great Britain (UK): Governed independently post-Brexit under the GB-CLP Regulation and UK REACH, operating as a separate statutory chemical regulatory regime from the EU.
  • Domestic Indian Framework: Addressed primarily under the Manufacture, Storage and Import of Hazardous Chemical (MSIHC) Rules, 1989, alongside applicable factory and chemical management standards.

Technical Data Points Required to Compile an SDS

Authoring an accurate, audit-ready SDS relies on verified technical inputs from the manufacturer or analytical laboratory:

  1. Substance or Mixture Characterization: Accurate chemical names, CAS numbers, EC numbers, and concentration ranges for all ingredients contributing to health, physical, or environmental hazard classifications.
  2. Physical and Chemical Properties (Section 9): Determined parameters such as flash point, pH, boiling point, vapor pressure, and kinematic viscosity.
  3. Transport Classification (Section 14): Correlation with the IMDG Code (ocean transport) and IATA DGR (air cargo), including UN number, Proper Shipping Name (PSN), Class, Packing Group, and Marine Pollutant status where applicable.
  4. Toxicological and Ecotoxicological Information (Sections 11 & 12): Relevant endpoints (such as LD50/LC50 values and aquatic toxicity data) that support the hazard classification assigned in Section 2.

Common Compliance Pitfalls in Export Documentation

Carriers, freight forwarders, and dangerous goods inspectors frequently review safety documents for technical and internal consistency:

  • Section 2 vs. Section 14 Discrepancies: Classifying a formulation as non-hazardous under Section 2 while declaring a regulated Class 3 (Flammable) or Class 8 (Corrosive) UN number in Section 14 (or vice-versa) can result in clarification requests, carrier rejection, or shipment delays.
  • Conflating Enforceable Exposure Limits (Section 8): Presenting non-binding scientific recommendations as mandatory statutory limits, or omitting local regulatory exposure limits applicable in the destination country.
  • Outdated Legacy Formats: Relying on pre-GHS 8- or 9-section formats that lack standardized GHS hazard pictograms, signal words, Hazard (H) statements, and Precautionary (P) statements.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

Is an SDS mandatory for chemical exports from India?

Requirements depend on the chemical, its hazard classification, destination-country regulations, customer specifications, and applicable transport rules. Hazardous chemicals commonly require an SDS for workplace safety communication and international compliance.

Can the same SDS be used for every country?

Not necessarily. An SDS frequently requires jurisdiction-specific adjustments for hazard classification thresholds, occupational exposure limits, regional inventory statuses, emergency contacts, and local regulatory references.

Does every SDS need 16 sections?

The 16-section format is the internationally recognized structure used across most GHS-based regulatory systems, although specific content, annexes, and legal provisions vary by jurisdiction.

What information is needed to prepare an SDS?

Typically, the product composition, CAS numbers, physical and chemical properties, intended industrial/commercial use, toxicological and ecotoxicological data, and available transport classifications are required.
